Putting the same product into one group

The problems

  1. Titles mix marketing copy, pack counts, parallel-import tags and other noise
  2. Brand name missing on 9.1% of listings
  3. The size field carries a value on only 38.2%
  4. A large number of groups
  5. No ground truth (no target value)

Two business rules

  • Pack count does not define a product
    A single bar and a 6-pack are the same product
  • Brand, unit size and origin together define identity
    Lemon and rose must stay apart

03 · Preprocessing05

One script, one output

Data preprocessing

  • Normalise how things are written: full-width to half-width, one letter case, whitespace stripped — so "１００Ｇ" and "100g" become one token
  • Strip marketing words: parallel import, local stock, random packaging, expiry date and so on
  • Parse seven fields out of the title and body text: EAN-13 barcode, brand, unit size, unit of measure, pack count, origin, product form
  • Discover the words that distinguish variants: surface rare terms like rose, lemon and aloe (via TF-IDF)

EAN-13: the common 13-digit global trade item number

A manufacturer assigns one globally unique thirteen-digit number to every size of every product. Numbers are issued per tradeable unit, which means a single bar and a 6-pack of the same soap also differ.

check digit = (10 − (odd×1 + even×3) mod 10) mod 10
  • A random 13-digit string passes the check only 1 time in 10
  • 13-digit strings pulled from sku_id pass at 82.7% (229/277), far above 10%, so almost all really are barcodes
  • By Bayes' rule: 97.67%–100% of the check-passing 13-digit strings are valid EAN-13 barcodes
    The lower end assumes non-barcodes pass by luck 1 time in 10; the less random they are, the closer to 100%
  • Across all listing fields, 190 distinct check-passing barcodes covering 273 listings (18.5%)

Merchants mistype, so a barcode is high-confidence evidence rather than ground truth.

04 · Pipeline06

Shared by all five

Shortlist → score → group

STEP 01

Shortlist: who is worth comparing

1,472 listings make 1.08 million pairs, and comparing all of them is slow and error-prone. Five channels run in parallel to surface likely matches; a hit on any one is enough.

STEP 02

Score every pair

Each shortlisted pair gets a score from 0 to 1. The scorer is a gradient-boosted decision tree (GBDT).

STEP 03

Group, with hard vetoes

Pairs above the threshold link into groups. Different unit size, conflicting origin, clearly different scent: any of these vetoes the merge outright.

1,472 listings → roughly 1,060–1,140 groups06

Why run five in parallel: with a single channel, one blank or mistyped field (origin, say) is enough to strand a listing so it never pairs with anything. Five channels cover for each other, so the approach holds up when the data is imperfect.

05 · Step two · Scoring08

Step 02 · Gradient-boosted decision trees

Scoring: gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDT), voting together

Two guards against self-deception

  • Five-fold cross-validation.
  • Every listing under one barcode must land in the same fold, so listings sharing a barcode are never split across train and test.

Why GBDT and not something else

ModelOut-of-fold AUC
GBDT 180 trees / depth 20.9762
GBDT 180 trees / depth 3 (in use)0.9757
GBDT 400 trees / depth 40.9656
Logistic regression0.8858

Depth 2 wins on AUC by 0.0005 but loses end to end: 2.48% vs 1.24% wrong merges, 88.5% vs 93.7% precision (measured at the then-fixed 0.98). What ships is the pipeline, not the ranker.

06 · Step three · Grouping09

Step 03

Grouping with hard vetoes: constrained clustering

How pairs become groups

0
Start. Every SKU is its own group of one. ABCD…
1
Must-links merge first — identical barcode — ahead of every scored edge, so a growing cluster can never keep two identical barcodes apart. They still face Test 1; they skip Test 2.
2
Then every scored pair ≥ threshold, best first. For edge (A,B), try to merge the two groups those listings currently sit in — both gates must pass:
Test 1 · compatibility every cross-pair clears hard_veto: unit, form, origin, formulation %, bundle, variant conflict
Test 2 · average linkage the mean score across all cross-pairs is itself ≥ threshold

Worked example · same soap, three listings · threshold 0.98

1
Best edge A–B at 0.99 — two singletons, nothing to average → {A,B}
2
Edge B–C at 0.99 — try {A,B} + {C}. Cross-pairs 0.99 and 0.97, no veto; mean 0.98 ≥ 0.98 → {A,B,C}

A–C on its own (0.97) never clears the bar. Average linkage is what admits it: the threshold governs whole clusters, not individual pairs.

Step 2 is swept over a fixed grid — 0.50 · 0.60 · 0.70 · 0.80 · 0.85 · 0.90 · 0.95 · 0.98 · 0.99 — and the release gate picks the operating point.

08 · A counter-intuitive result12

Measure first, conclude second

"More similar photos mean the same product" is false

In practice, different variants of one product line always share a single packaging design. So structural image similarity runs higher on exactly the listings that are hardest to tell apart.

Naive similarity: shape signals below random

Image descriptorAUC
Greyscale structure0.419
Hue histogram0.601
dhash normalised similarity0.425

0.5 is random. Both shape descriptors sit below it — the more alike, the likelier these are different scents from one line. Only hue (0.601) helps a little, since a new scent usually means new colours.

The clean signal: reused photos

dhash distanceTrue pairs hitFalse pairs hit
≤ 013.0%0 / 645
≤ 2 · in use17.1%2 / 645
≤ 417.6%13 / 645

Resellers of the same product routinely reuse the manufacturer's photo. Re-uploaded, the filename differs but the pixels barely do, and that is the usable evidence.

All three image signals go to the model unsigned; it decides how to weigh them12
09 · Metric definitions13

Six metrics

MetricIn plain termsDirection
Completeness (recall)Of 193 pairs that should merge, how many were foundhigher is better
Wrong-merge rateOf 645 pairs that should not merge, how many were mergedlower is better · the damage metric
Correctness (precision)Share of merge decisions that were correcthigher is better
Pack invarianceOf 85 pairs differing only in pack count, how many were groupedbusiness rule · must be ~100%
Adversarial regressionSeven hand-verified real failure cases, how many passmust be 7/7
Release gateAll of the above must hold before anything shipsany single failure blocks

Why a gate at all: recall is easy to inflate by merging more (merge everything into one group and recall is 100%). So the rules come first: wrong merges ≤ 2.5%, pack invariance ≥ 98%, all seven cases pass. Only then, among the approaches within 2pp of the best recall, does precision decide.

Pack invariance is a regression test for a business rule, not a performance metric13
